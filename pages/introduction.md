## Introduction

*Ratatouille*, a movie about a rat who lives under Paris, is the 78th highest grossing film in the history of the Disney franchise. In an introductory scene, Remy, the rat and main character, climbs a roof to a landscape view of Paris. You, the viewer, only know he is in Paris because of the Eiffel Tower. Nothing else in the scene is distinctly Parisian. Constructed for the Exposition Universelle in 1889, the Eiffel Tower is seared into our collective consciousness. It is also a testament to the work of World’s Fairs, over a half century, to change how we, as humans, see the world. Indeed, everything around us is intimately and directly linked to the business of collection and display that was at the center of  World’s Fairs between 1851 and 1904. Highway and transportation systems. Food services. Agricultural policies. Theme park experiences. Museum experiences. Sports mascots. The conspicuous consumption of consumer goods. The organization of K-12 and higher education courses and textbooks. The wealth of Boston and Portland. The increasingly fine lines between illusion and reality. The list goes on. 

*A World on Display: Anthropology and World’s Fairs, 1851-1904* was organized and curated by the twenty students enrolled in the spring 2025 University of Southern Maine course ANT 320: Anthropology and the Museum, taught by Dr. David Shane Lowry, Assistant Professor of Anthropology in the Department of Geography-Anthropology. Professor Lowry, alongside Dr. Libby Bischof, Executive Director of the Osher Map Library and Smith Center for Cartographic Education and Professor of History, guided and assisted the students in the organization and curation of the exhibition. 
At the start of the semester, based on the strength of the materials available at the Osher Map Library, the students chose to work across five World’s Fair groupings: International World’s Fairs (Paris 1889 and 1900 and London 1851), The Centennial Exposition (Philadephia 1876), The Columbian Exposition (Chicago 1893), The Pan-American Exposition (Buffalo 1901), and the Louisiana Purchase Exposition (St. Louis 1904). In both larger and smaller groups, we took the co-curation process seriously. Throughout the semester we collaboratively engaged – across classroom and library spaces in Portland and Gorham (and in dozens and dozens of emails) – in every aspect of exhibition planning and design: brainstorming sessions, defining and refining themes, the selection of materials, deep research and analysis, spirited discussion, label writing, design, and, ultimately, during the last week of the sixteen-week semester, installation and the exhibition opening.  

As an important aspect of Professor Lowry’s Anthropology and the Museum course, something that we highlight throughout this exhibit is the formation of the discipline of “Anthropology” as a set of intellectual and political tools used within this system of land theft and associated reductions of Indigenous peoples into toys and trinkets. Anthropology’s hyper focus on particular “cultures” and its work, especially in period of these World’s Fairs, to condense complicated human realities into consumable snacks, was a distraction (and, often, remains a distraction) from violence against Indigenous peoples across the imperial landscape of the United States. Nearly all of these Fairs, especially in the United States, were set against the backdrop of the lead-up to and aftermath of the Spanish American War of 1898. 

As educators, we feel it is important to note that this exhibit is planned by and curated with students at the University of Southern Maine. In a world of Instagram and other mediums of technological influence, it is important to examine the legacy of World’s Fairs with students who have never known a world without Internet connections. Between the mid-1800s and the early-1900s, World’s Fairs were organized to distort reality and to distract through engineering of fantasy via collections and display. Those episodes of distraction and distortion have now been etched into the microchips and algorithms that power social media. Such comparisons have not been lost on all of us.  

We invite you to journey through the various items that we have curated. Our purpose is not to, yet again, create a space of distraction and distortion. Rather, in this joint venture, we hope that you (the visitor and passerby) will begin to understand the weight of the World’s Fairs as something current, and not just in the past. We ask you to look more closely. We ask you to ask better questions. These World’s Fairs, as you will see, ought to be as significant to the peoples on Wabanaki lands as they are to the peoples in Philadelphia, Chicago, Buffalo, St. Louis, London and Paris.
