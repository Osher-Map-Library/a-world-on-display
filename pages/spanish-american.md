## The Spanish American War and American Imperialism

The collections of the Osher Map Library and Smith Center for Cartographic Education contain numerous maps and other cartographic representations pertaining to the Spanish American War. One of the reasons we have so many maps from this era is that mapmaking was central to the Imperial project, a key way for states and nations to “officially” lay claim to  territories beyond their borders. The Spanish American War, a conflict fought between Spain and the United States between April and August of 1898, formally began with the sinking of the U.S.S. Maine in Havana Harbor (Cuba) in February of 1898, the result of a “mysterious explosion” blamed on Spain (and later discovered to have been an internal fire in the ship’s coal bunker that set off stored ammunition on board). Newspapers made much of the ship’s destruction, and cries of “Remember the Maine,” helped pressure President McKinley and Congress to declare war on Spain. By the end of the brief war, fought mostly in the Carribbean and the Pacific, Spain’s nearly-four century colonial presence in the Americas had come to an end, and the victorious United States took on the islands of Cuba, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines. Twenty-two years after celebrating the centennial of the political independence of the United States from the British Empire at the Philadelphia World’s Fair, America had itself become an empire, which was not without controversy both in the United States and the wider world. There is much to say about the Spanish American War and American Imperialism, but for the purposes of this exhibition, its legacy in terms of World’s Fairs and Exhibitions, particularly in the United States, cannot be underestimated, especially in Buffalo (1901) and St. Louis (1904). 

The title of this exhibit, “A World on Display,” speaks to the very underpinnings of the ways in which President McKinley and others in favor of building out America’s empire beyond the borders of the United States, justified the possession of nations such as the Philippines and Hawai’i (annexed in 1898, separate from the Spanish American War). Namely–that the territories the United States claimed were, in McKinley’s own words, “unfit for self government,” and therefore needed the United States to “educate the Filipinos, and uplift and civilize and Christianize them….” This “civilizing project,” what Bristish poet Rudyard Kipling referred to as the “White Man’s Burden” in a poem he wrote for Americans in 1899, was also the work of World’s Fairs and Exhibitions. As you will see throughout our exhibit, students have selected examples from numerous world’s fairs that highlight the ways in which Anthropologists, Politicians, Educators, Industrialists, and Fair Organizers worked together to to showcase American “progress,” while simultaneously and falsely claiming the inferiority of the Filipino people and other non-White peoples of the world, including Native Americans being forced from their homelands throughout the United States. One of the ways in which McKinley and others worked to claim these territories for the United States was, in his own words, to have them “put on the map.” Once they were visualized as part of the United States, along with Hawai’i, then they, in turn, became part of the United States, whether the sovereign peoples of those territories, only briefly free from Spanish rule, wanted to or not.

###  A World on Display–Literally and Figuratively

{% include feature/image.html objectid="awod_12;awod_14" %}

The two maps featured at the start of “A World on Display: Anthropology and World’s Fairs, 1851-1904,” were both produced in conjunction with World’s Fairs and Exhibitions. James Wyld, a prominent London cartographer and map publisher, produced a new version of his “Plan of London" to coincide with the opening of the Great Exhibit of the Works of Industry of All Nations, held at London’s newly built Crystal Palace, in 1851. The Crystal Palace, site of the first World’s Fair, is featured prominently on the bottom border of the map, and the map’s title is helpfully given in multiple languages in order to assist foreign visitors in town for the Exhibit. They could also fold and unfold the map, which could fit in one’s pocket, to consult their transportation options in terms of ferries, train stations, and bridges across the Thames. Wyld, no stranger to self-promotion, also included one of his newest ventures on the map – “Mr. Wyld’s Model of the Earth in Leicester Square” – a special dome that included a 19-meter sphere of the Earth. Visitors could step inside a view of a scale model of the Earth made from plaster of Paris. Wyld intentionally opened this attraction in 1851, and it became a popular tourist attraction for fair visitors. 

On the other hand, Levi W. Yaggy, a Chicago map publisher who specialized in pedagogical maps and views made for schools and classrooms, produced a completely new edition of his popular chromolithographic map set “Yaggy’s Geographical Portfolio,” in 1893, in conjunction with the Chicago World’s Fair. In his Climactic Chart of the World, like many Geographers of the time, Yaggy organized and classified the earth’s flora and fauna (including human races) according to the five Aristotelian zones: two “frigid zones” at the poles, unfavorable to most life; the lush equatorial “torrid zone;” and, in between, the two Northern and Southern “temperate zones.” The northern temperate zone was the home of so-called “civilization,” of learning and industry. The southern temperate zone was considered by imperialists to be a “proper” site for colonies of White settlement. In imperialist arguments made at the turn-of-the-century, such regions were thus thought to be best subjected to White control via local rulers. While Yaggy and other Geographers and Cartographers organized and categorized the peoples of the Earth into fixed and hierarchical categories, World’s Fair organizers and Anthropologists organized people and products into equally fixed and rigid structures. As you wander through the exhibit, we invite you to think about the ways in which those fixed and invented categories remain with us in the present day.
