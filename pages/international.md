## The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations: London, 1851 

The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations of 1851, more commonly called The Crystal Palace Exhibition, is known for being the first World’s Fair. In 1851, London’s newly built Crystal Palace, designed by Joseph Paxton, was one of the most impressive architectural feats of the time. Constructed from walls of glass and cast iron, the massive building became an integral aspect of the fair as it housed all of the fair’s exhibitions. Physically located in Hyde Park, London, England, the fair was held between May 1st and October 15th, and more than six million visitors attended. After the fair closed, the Crystal Palace was disassembled and relocated to Sydenham Hill in South London, where subsequent fairs and international events were held. Sadly, in 1936, the building burned to the ground.

{% include feature/image.html objectid="awod_13" with="75" %}

The fair at Crystal Palace was a request by Prince Albert (1819-1861), the Prince Consort, and Queen Victoria (1819-1901), who wanted to create an exhibition to celebrate British industry, technology, design, manufacturing, exports, and other achievements. At the time, the fair housed the largest display of goods ever gathered in one place, organized into four main categories: Raw Material, Machinery and Mechanical Inventions, Manufactured Products, and Fine Arts. Other key people in the production of this fair included Joseph Paxton (1801-1865), the lead architect and horticulturist, and Henry Cole (1808-1882), a member of the Royal Commission of 1851. Not only did these men seek to show off Britain's industrial accomplishments and new technologies within the exhibition, they also encouraged international trade and cooperation. Their intention was to promote new technological and manufacturing skills — while at the same time creating a “global union.” By presenting a variety of advanced machines, tools, and other technological products, Britain demonstrated their industrial power and leadership over other nations, both economically and technologically as well as culturally. This exhibition not only inspired the people of England, but also inventors, engineers, artists, and anthropologists from around the world. 

In reality, however, the “global union” promoted at the Great Exhibition was more like a show-and-tell of the British Empire’s imperialist  conquests. The fair featured a plethora of exhibitions dedicated to the British Colonies, many of which dehumanized Indigenous communities across the world, stripping them of authentic representation, and humiliating them on a global stage. The Great Exhibition’s experiences were shaped through a colonial lens that granted permission to the fair’s organizers to promote British dominance over non-western cultures. As a result of this exhibition, the Crystal Palace quickly became a symbol of Imperialism and modern engineering, highlighting the shift from an agricultural to an industrial society that became a central focus of the fairs that followed, both in Europe and the United States.

## Exposition Universelle: Paris, France, 1889

The 1889 Paris World’s Fair, also known as the Exposition Universelle de 1889, took place from May 6th to November 6th, 1889. The Fair was organized as the centennial celebration of the Storming of the Bastille in 1789, and the beginning of the French Revolution (1789-1799). Due to the fair’s theme of celebration regarding the overthrow of the French monarchy, many monarchies in Europe chose to not officially participate in the fair, although independent companies and individuals from those countries did exhibit and attend with private sponsors. The 1889 World’s Fair covered about 235 acres and spread across two central locations. It attracted roughly 32,000,000 attendees over the course of six months. While this fair featured several displays of technological and architectural feats, the focal point was the construction of The Eiffel tower, which became an enduring symbol of the fair and of the city of Paris.

{% include feature/image.html objectid="awod_15" with="75" %}

Designed by engineer and entrepreneur, Gustave Eiffel, the construction of the Eiffel tower officially started in January of 1887 and received significant backlash from those who opposed the building. In a series of letters titled “Protest Against Mr. Eiffel’s Tower” published in the Le Temps newspaper, several notable figures in the Paris art world shared their distaste for the design of the tower, citing concern for the Parisian image. Despite the opposition, construction of the tower moved forward and was completed in two and a half years; it was finished just over a month before the fair was set to open. The Eiffel tower, the largest tower in the world at the time of its completion, garnered nearly 2,000,000 million visitors during the 1889 fair (it cost an extra five francs to climb). The tower attracted several big name visitors during the fair, including the American wild west showman, Buffalo Bill. 

Buffalo Bill and sharpshooter Annie Oakley held a popular American “Wild West” exhibition at the 1889 World’s Fair. This exhibition was meant to demonstrate the “taming of the Wild West,” and perpetuated the image of Native peoples as “savages” and white men as both “saviours” and “victims.” The show included several figures on horseback as they ran through scenes of ambushes, wars, the [over]hunting of the plains buffalo, and Buffalo Bill saving the day. According to many accounts, the French narration of the show was very poor, and left the French-speaking audience confused before they were won over by Oakley’s sharpshooting presentation. Overall, the Wild West show had a very positive reception among the French and international audiences in Paris, which came with the cost of perpetuating harmful stereotypes about Native peoples and amplifying the narrative that the already well established Native lands and ways of life in the West needed to be stolen and eradicated to ensure American “progress” and expansion in the Western Plains. Buffalo Bill’s Wild West show traveled throughout Europe and the United States between 1883 and 1913, including popular stints at other world’s fairs such as Chicago (1893), Buffalo (1901), and St. Louis (1904).

## Exposition Universelle: Paris, France, 1900

The second Paris World’s Fair, which was also referred to as the “Exposition Universelle,” was held from April 15th, 1900 to November 15th, 1900. It took place all over Paris. Key locations included the Champ-de-Mars, the Trocadero, and the Esplanade des Invalides. The 1900 Exposition Universelle was the largest World's Fair to date, covering more than 543 acres and welcoming  over 51 million visitors during the six months it was open. This fair was significant for the people of Paris and the city itself. To celebrate, the Eiffel Tower (built for the 1889 Fair) was painted gold, the first and only time this color was used on the iconic monument.

{% include feature/image.html objectid="assets/img/le_palais_ponsin.jpg" alt="Souvenir image of Le Palais Ponsin lit up with onlookers at the Worlds Fair" caption='Page from <a href="/items/awod_17.html">Souvenir de Exposition 1900</a>.' width="75" %}

One of the main attractions of the 1900 Paris Fair was “The Palace of Electricity.” This exhibit included five thousand multi-colored incandescent lamps that illuminated the Paris night sky. This was a monumental achievement because for most people at this fair it was the first time they saw something lit up at night. Another significant technological innovation and attraction was the debut of the Paris Metro. Fulgence Bienvenue, an engineer, was behind the design, and in only four years he created one of the most efficient public transit systems in the world. Construction for the Metro began in 1896 and was completed by July 1900, further highlighting France’s  proficiency in technology and engineering. The 1900 Paris Fair was significant from a cultural standpoint as well. It marked  the first time a summer Olympic games was held outside of Greece. Paris did a lot to accommodate the games, including the erection of buildings such as the Velodrome for cycling events. 

The Exposition Universelle revealed the deep contradictions of the era. Alongside exhibitions celebrating Black intellectual achievement in the United States–curated by W.E.B. Du Bois and Thomas J. Calloway to showcase the progress of African Americans–stood human zoos and colonial villages; stark reminders of the pervasive racism and Imperialist mindset of the time in Europe and the United States. While colonial villages and so-called "human zoos" attempted to dehumanize Indigenous peoples from various colonized lands, a powerful counter-narrative emerged in DuBois’ and Calloway’s “American Negro Exhibit.” This groundbreaking display brought over 500 photographs, charts, books, and artifacts to Paris to document the achievements, resilience, and intellectual life of African Americans just decades after emancipation. At a fair that often distorted and marginalized non-European cultures, this  exhibit stood as a bold assertion: Black Americans were not curiosities of the past, but active architects of the future. Native Americans and other Indigenous peoples were also placed in  complicated roles at the fair. While some participated in ethnographic displays, often under degrading and exploitative conditions that portrayed Indigenous life as primitive, other Native performers and artists used the international stage to preserve cultural practices, resist stereotypes, and assert their survival amidst global policies aimed at erasing them.
