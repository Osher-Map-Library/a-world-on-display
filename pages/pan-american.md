##  The Pan-American Exposition: Buffalo, New York, 1901

The Pan-American Exposition was held in Buffalo, New York, in 1901. The fair ran for six months and welcomed more than 8 million guests between May and November. Covering 350 acres of Delaware Park, the fairgrounds featured extravagant architecture like the Niagara Falls Electric Tower, whose novel innovations quite literally lit up the night. There were many temporary buildings constructed and dedicated to displaying various fields of research, as well as individual states and countries.  It also included hundreds of exhibits meant to promote commerce and innovation. The fair also had various stages for entertainment, including the Coliseum, the Temple of Music, and the Midway–a street lined with enticing cultural displays. Today, the Pan-American Exposition is best remembered for the showmanship of the Midway Day Parade, its technological feats, and the assassination of the United States' 25th President, William McKinley, which occurred at the fair on September 7th (he died of bullet wounds on September 14th). While these events and flashy exhibits made the fair a sight to behold, its true nature and purpose was much more sinister. The fair’s central purpose was to promote and amplify the United States’ emergence as a colonial power under the costume of promoting “good understanding” amongst the global West. 

The Pan-American Exposition was the first World’s Fair to take place after the Spanish- American War of 1898. Following the victory of the United States and its subsequent Imperialist and destructive conquest of territories such as the Philippines, a large portion of the fairgrounds was dedicated to introducing the American people to its “collection” of new territories. In order to do this, Fair organizers created what became known as “human zoos,” outdoor fenced off exhibits where cultural and ethnic groups were displayed and exploited for monetary gain and entertainment. The Midway, which showcased a variety of stereotypical examples of international cultures frozen in “primitive” times, also perpetuated these practices of exploitation. Such exhibits pushed ideologies of nationalist pride while also furthering themes of imperialism, racism, and pseudo-scientific superiority. 

The symbol of the Pan-American Exposition (located on the wall nearby)  depicts the United States, personified as Columbia, behaving as a “white savior” by reaching her hand to metaphorically pull South America from “savagery” and toward “Western” ideals of progress. These ideologies are directly reflected within the fair’s conceptualizations of Imperial power at the turn of the century. In November of 1901, the fair ended with a choreographed battle at the Coliseum. This grand-closing sham battle between U.S. Buffalo troops and villainized Indigenous actors positioned America as the “victors,” both physically and metaphorically, in the eyes of spectators who sat and watched on stolen Seneca land. Despite the fanfare and marketing, fair organizers ultimately lost money due to their inability to cover the costs of the extravagant displays. The only permanent structure left from the fair now serves as the Buffalo History Museum, thus memorializing the controversial history of the United States and the Imperial spoils of the Spanish-American war for years to come.
