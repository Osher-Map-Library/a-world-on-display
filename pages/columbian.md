## The Columbian Exposition: Chicago, Illinois, 1893

### Humanity on Display: The 1893 Chicago World's Fair and the Invention of Difference

On May 1st, 1893, with the push of a button, President Grover Cleveland kicked off the Chicago World’s Fair and thus marked a definitive turn in American industrialization and progress. The Chicago World’s Fair, also known as the Columbian Exposition, was held from May 1st to October 31st, 1893, to commemorate and celebrate the 400th anniversary of Christopher Columbus’s arrival in the Americas. It became one of the most visited and influential public events in American history, with over 27 million people attending during its six-month run. Known for its emphasis on entertainment, technology, and the arrangement of Indigenous peoples, Chicago’s 1893 Exposition is perhaps the most iconic of America’s fairs. The debuts of the “moving sidewalk” and the first-ever Ferris wheel (or “Chicago Wheel”) are among numerous technologies introduced at this fair that we take for granted today. With its heavy emphasis on the consumption of goods, this fair also marked a definitive turn towards Western industrialization and the birth of modern capitalism. While the fair’s overall theme was “progress,” it was defined from a Western, industrialized point of view.

Beyond and beneath the grand buildings, electric lights, and new inventions showcased at the fair was a more violent reality: human beings were also put on display alongside these consumer goods and technological feats. Throughout the fairgrounds, Indigenous and foreign peoples were objectified in racist exhibits designed to separate people by race, culture, and Western ideas of “civilization.” Human beings enclosed within “ethnological villages” (also referred to as “human zoos”) were made to perform for the public. These displays were not created to understand and treat people with respect, but rather to compare them in ways that suggested some were more “advanced” than others. These dehumanizing displays treated people as objects to be studied and looked at rather than as individuals with their own stories and dignity.

Anthropology, the study of human cultures, was still a new academic field in 1893. Many of the early anthropologists, including Franz Boas, the so-called "Father of American Anthropology," curated exhibits on display in Chicago or visited the fair. The ideas about race and culture put forth by Anthropologists shaped and influenced the fair’s exhibits, and the many Anthropology and History museums founded after the fair. Many items on display in the anthropological exhibits in Chicago–including human remains–were sent to these newly founded museums across the country, including the Field Museum in Chicago, which was created just after the fair closed. Racist ideas from the fair continue to influence how cultures and histories are displayed to the public today.

By examining how the 1893 Chicago World’s Fair organized and displayed people, we are invited to ask new questions: Who gets to represent humanity? Who is allowed to speak for themselves? And what does it mean when cultures are turned into exhibits?
