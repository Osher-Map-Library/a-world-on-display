## The Louisiana Purchase Exposition: St. Louis, Missouri, 1904

The St. Louis World’s Fair of 1904 was organized as a centennial celebration of Thomas Jefferson’s 1803 Louisiana Purchase (and the subsequent Lewis and Clark Expedition of 1804-1806), events that marked the beginning of Manifest Destiny (the westward colonial expansion of the United States). The St. Louis fairgrounds covered 1,200 acres of Forest Park and featured nearly 1,500 buildings constructed for the fair, including the Palace of Fine Arts (now the St. Louis Art Museum). The fair attracted an estimated 20 million visitors over the course of seven months in 1904; visitors primarily arrived by train to view thousands of objects, artworks, and exhibits on display from 62 different countries. As the world’s largest international exposition to date, the expansive layout of the fair paralleled American attitudes of expansionism at the turn-of-the-century. 

By 1904, American exceptionalism had reached its crux. The United States significantly expanded its empire after their 1898 victory in the Spanish-American War, annexing the territories of Guam, Puerto Rico, Cuba, and the Philippines (formerly held by Spain). The United States also formally annexed the Kingdom of Hawai’i in 1898, an effort largely organized by Maine politician and founder of Maine’s Republican party, John Leavitt Stevens, who served as the American Minister to Hawai’i until 1893, when he orchestrated the overthrow of the Hawai’ian Kingdom that ultimately led to annexation. Due to the internal controversy within the United States over the recent militaristic and imperialistic actions of the government, the organizers of the Louisiana Purchase Exposition arranged exhibits at the World’s Fair that sought to justify the expansionist policies of the U.S. government, particularly the colonization of the Philippines and the attempted conquests of Native American nations. Anthropologists also played a large role in the organization of the fair, particularly in how the industrial exhibits were used to demonstrate the idea of American exceptionalism. 

The Louisiana Purchase Exposition was organized to encourage average fairgoers to be inspired by the industrialization and innovation of the United States. The largest buildings were those showcasing Agriculture, Transportation, Machinery, Manufacturing, Electricity, and Fine Arts. These so-called palaces contrasted with the exhibits that featured dehumanizing displays of nonwestern cultures meant to further illustrate white superiority. One of the largest exhibits among them was the Philippine village. Spanning 40 acres of the fairgrounds, the village held and displayed over 1,000 Indigenous Filipinos, whose culture was portrayed as “savage” and “uncivilized.” For many Americans, the St. Louis World’s Fair was their first introduction to America’s newly conquered territories outside of the U.S. and Europe. While there were displays from all over the world, the “primitive” representations of nonwestern Indigenous cultures were used to justify American occupation of its newly established colonies – including the colony of America itself. This celebration of American Imperialism at the World’s Fair served as a distraction from the U.S. government’s elaborate and ongoing efforts to steal Native American lands, while displacing and disenfranchising Natives in the process.
